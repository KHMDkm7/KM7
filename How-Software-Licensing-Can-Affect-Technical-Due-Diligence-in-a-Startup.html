How Software Licensing Can Affect Technical Due Diligence in a Startup

A startup can build an impressive product using a combination of proprietary code, open-source libraries, commercial software, and third-party services. But having access to technology does not always mean the company has unrestricted ownership or usage rights.

Software licensing can therefore become an important part of technical due diligence. Investors and acquirers may need to understand what technology the startup owns, what it licenses, and whether its use of third-party software creates obligations or restrictions.

Why Software Licensing Matters

When investors evaluate a startup's technology, they are also evaluating the rights attached to that technology.

A product may depend on hundreds of external software components. Some may be freely available under open-source licenses, while others may require commercial subscriptions or impose specific conditions on redistribution and modification.

Unclear licensing can create uncertainty around the technology's long-term use and ownership.

Open-Source Software Is Common in Startups

Open-source software allows startups to build products without developing every component from scratch.

Frameworks, libraries, databases, operating systems, and development tools can significantly reduce development time. Using open-source software is not inherently a problem during technical due diligence.

The important consideration is whether the startup understands the licenses attached to the components it uses and is complying with their requirements.

Different Licenses Create Different Obligations

Open-source licenses are not all identical.

Some licenses allow broad commercial use with relatively limited obligations. Others can impose additional requirements when software is modified or distributed.

A startup should therefore maintain visibility into the licenses associated with important dependencies instead of assuming that every open-source component can be used in exactly the same way.

Dependency Management Can Become Difficult

As a product grows, the number of software dependencies can increase substantially.

Some dependencies may themselves rely on additional packages, creating a chain of components that can become difficult to track manually.

Outdated dependencies can also introduce security and maintenance concerns. A startup may eventually need to upgrade or replace components that have become unsupported.

Commercial Software Can Create Recurring Obligations

Not every external technology is open source.

Startups may rely on commercial databases, monitoring platforms, development tools, cloud services, analytics products, and other licensed software.

During due diligence, investors may want to understand which licenses are critical to operating the product and what costs or restrictions are associated with them.

Contractor-Built Software Needs Clear Ownership

Licensing and ownership can become more complicated when external developers contribute to the product.

A startup may have hired freelancers, agencies, or contractors to build specific features or entire sections of the application. Founders should maintain clear agreements covering intellectual property and the rights associated with the resulting work.

This is particularly important when different vendors have contributed to different parts of the same product.

Third-Party APIs Are Also Dependencies

Software licensing is only one part of dependency management.

Third-party APIs can become essential to product functionality even when the startup does not own the underlying service.

Payment providers, authentication systems, communication platforms, data providers, and other external services can all introduce contractual or operational dependencies.

Founders should understand which services are business-critical and what would happen if their pricing, terms, functionality, or availability changed.

Technical Debt Can Make Dependency Management Harder

Technical debt can accumulate around outdated libraries and poorly documented dependencies.

A development team may continue using an older component because upgrading it would require changes across several parts of the application. Over time, this can make modernization increasingly difficult.

During a technical due diligence startup review, these dependencies may become visible as future engineering costs.

Build a Software Dependency Inventory

Founders can reduce uncertainty by maintaining an inventory of important software dependencies.

  • Software or library name
  • Current version
  • License type
  • Purpose within the product
  • Business criticality
