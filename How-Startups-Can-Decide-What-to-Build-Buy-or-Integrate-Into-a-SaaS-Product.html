How Startups Can Decide What to Build, Buy, or Integrate Into a SaaS Product

When building a SaaS product, founders rarely need to create every capability from scratch. Authentication, payments, email delivery, analytics, file storage, search, and other capabilities can often be purchased or connected through existing services.

The challenge is deciding which parts should become proprietary product functionality and which should rely on external tools. Making that decision early can affect development speed, costs, technical complexity, and long-term control.

Start With the Product's Core Value

The first question should be whether a capability is central to why customers use the product.

If a particular function represents the product's main competitive value, building it internally may provide more control over how it works and evolves. If it is simply supporting infrastructure, using an established service may be more practical.

For example, a SaaS company developing a specialized financial workflow may need to build its core workflow while using an external provider for payment processing.

Identify What the Product Must Control

Some capabilities require a high degree of control because they directly affect the product experience or business model.

Consider whether the startup needs control over:

  • Core business logic
  • Customer-facing workflows
  • Data models
  • Pricing logic
  • Product-specific algorithms
  • Important reporting functionality

The more central a capability is to the product, the more carefully the team should evaluate whether outsourcing it could limit future development.

Consider Buying Commodity Functionality

Some capabilities are necessary but do not differentiate the product.

Examples include:

  • Email delivery
  • Payment processing
  • Cloud storage
  • Authentication
  • Error tracking
  • Monitoring

Building these systems internally can require significant engineering and operational effort. If established providers already offer the required functionality, purchasing the capability may allow the team to focus engineering resources on the product itself.

Evaluate Existing Integrations

An integration can be useful when the product needs functionality that another service already provides.

Before selecting an integration, review:

  • API capabilities
  • Authentication requirements
  • Data access
  • Rate limits
  • Pricing
  • Reliability
  • Documentation
  • Support for required workflows

A service may offer the right feature but still be unsuitable if its API cannot support the specific way the SaaS product needs to use it.

Compare Development Cost With Long-Term Cost

The decision should not be based only on the initial development cost.

Building a capability creates ongoing responsibilities for maintenance, security, infrastructure, testing, and future improvements.

Buying or integrating a service creates a different cost structure that may include subscriptions, usage-based pricing, integration work, and dependency management.

Compare both the initial implementation effort and the expected cost of maintaining the capability over time.

Consider Time to Market

Development time can be an important factor for an early-stage SaaS product.

If an established provider can handle a non-core capability reliably, integrating it may allow the team to reach users sooner.

However, speed should not automatically override architectural considerations. A quick integration that creates major limitations later may result in additional work that outweighs the initial time savings.

Evaluate Technical Complexity

Some capabilities look simple from the outside but require substantial engineering underneath.

For example, building a payment system involves much more than creating a payment form. It may require transaction handling, webhooks, refunds, failed payments, security controls, reconciliation, and subscription management.

Before deciding to build something internally, estimate the full technical scope rather than only the visible functionality.

Consider Security and Compliance Requirements

Security requirements can change the build-versus-buy decision.

If a provider already specializes in a sensitive capability, using its infrastructure may reduce the amount of functionality the startup needs to manage directly.

However, the team still needs to understand what data is being transferred, how it is protected, what permissions are required, and what responsibilities remain with the SaaS company.

Review Data Ownership

Before relying on an external service, determine where the product's data will live and which system is considered the source of truth.

Ask:

  • Who owns the data?
  • Can the data be exported?
  • How quickly can it be retrieved?
  • What happens if the service is discontinued?
  • Can the product migrate to another provider?

These questions become particularly important when the external service stores information that is essential to the product.

Consider Vendor Lock-In

Using an external service can make the product dependent on that provider.

Vendor lock-in becomes a concern when switching providers would require substantial application changes, data migration, or customer disruption.

Not every dependency needs to be avoided. The important thing is to understand which dependencies are difficult to replace and whether that level of dependency is acceptable.

Evaluate Scalability and Pricing

An external service that is affordable at low usage may become considerably more expensive as the SaaS product grows.

Review how pricing changes with:

  • Number of users
  • API requests
  • Storage
  • Transactions
  • Data processing
  • Feature usage

Estimate costs at several realistic usage levels rather than looking only at the initial pricing tier.

Decide What Should Be Built Internally

Building internally can make sense when a capability is strategically important, difficult to replace, or central to the customer experience.

It may also be appropriate when existing providers cannot meet important product requirements.

However, the decision should include the full cost of engineering, infrastructure, testing, maintenance, security, and future development.

Use a Hybrid Approach When Appropriate

The decision does not have to be entirely build or entirely buy.

A SaaS product can combine internal functionality with external services.

For example, the startup might build its proprietary subscription management experience while integrating an external payment processor. It might also build its own reporting interface while using a third-party analytics platform for underlying event collection.

This approach can provide control over important product functionality while reducing the amount of infrastructure the team needs to maintain.

Document the Decision

Once the team decides whether to build, buy, or integrate a capability, document the reasoning.

The decision record can include:

  • Capability being evaluated
  • Options considered
  • Expected development effort
  • Ongoing costs
  • Security considerations
  • Data ownership
  • Vendor dependencies
  • Reasons for the final approach

This gives future engineers and product leaders context when the decision needs to be revisited.

Review the Decision as the Product Grows

A build-versus-buy decision made for an early MVP may not remain appropriate indefinitely.

As usage increases, the startup may discover that an external service has become too expensive, too restrictive, or too difficult to customize.

Conversely, a capability that was initially built internally may become unnecessary to maintain if a reliable third-party solution becomes available.

Review important dependencies as the product, customer base, and technical requirements change.

Final Thoughts

Deciding what to build, buy, or integrate is ultimately about allocating engineering effort where it creates the most value.

Core product capabilities may require internal ownership, while commodity functionality can often be purchased or connected through established providers. The right decision depends on product importance, technical complexity, cost, security, data ownership, scalability, and long-term dependency.

Making these decisions deliberately can help a startup avoid both extremes: rebuilding functionality that already exists and outsourcing capabilities that should remain under the company's control.
