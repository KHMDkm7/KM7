How Startups Can Prepare Their Engineering Team for Technical Due Diligence

Technical due diligence examines more than a startup's code and infrastructure. The people responsible for building and maintaining the technology can also provide important insight into how the product operates, how technical decisions are made, and whether the company has enough engineering ownership to support its plans.

Founders should therefore prepare the engineering team before a technical review begins. The goal is not to coach employees into giving rehearsed answers. It is to make sure responsibilities, technical knowledge, documentation, and communication are clear.

Clarify Engineering Responsibilities

Start by documenting who is responsible for the major technical areas of the business.

This may include:

  • Application development
  • Infrastructure
  • Database management
  • Security
  • Deployments
  • Testing
  • Technical architecture
  • Third-party integrations

Clear ownership makes it easier for a reviewer to understand how the engineering organization operates.

Identify Critical Technical Knowledge

Determine which parts of the product are understood by only one or two people.

Critical knowledge may include:

  • Production infrastructure
  • Database operations
  • Core business logic
  • Deployment procedures
  • Authentication systems
  • Important integrations

Knowledge concentration is not automatically a problem. However, the company should recognize where it exists and reduce unnecessary dependence through documentation and knowledge sharing.

Review the Engineering Team Structure

Investors may want to understand whether the current engineering organization is appropriate for the startup's stage.

Review:

  • Team size
  • Technical leadership
  • Developer responsibilities
  • External development support
  • Hiring plans
  • Areas where additional expertise is required

The objective is not to have a large engineering team. It is to determine whether the existing team can reasonably maintain the product and execute the technical roadmap.

Make Technical Leadership Clear

A startup should have a clear answer to the question of who owns major technical decisions.

Depending on the company, this could be a CTO, technical founder, engineering lead, or another designated technical owner.

Unclear technical authority can create confusion around architecture, engineering priorities, and risk management.

Review Engineering Processes

Technical reviewers may ask how the team develops and releases software.

Make sure the team can explain the relevant processes around:

  • Planning
  • Code reviews
  • Testing
  • Deployments
  • Bug management
  • Incident response
  • Technical documentation

These processes do not need to resemble those of a large enterprise. They should be appropriate for the startup's size and product complexity.

Check Access and Ownership

Engineering team members should have appropriate access to the systems they need without creating unnecessary security exposure.

Review access to:

  • Source-code repositories
  • Cloud platforms
  • Production systems
  • Databases
  • Deployment tools
  • Third-party services

Former employees and contractors should not retain unnecessary access, and important company systems should not depend on personal accounts.

Prepare Developers to Explain the Product

Engineers who participate in technical due diligence should be able to explain the parts of the system they own.

They should understand:

  • What their systems do
  • How those systems interact with other components
  • Why important technical decisions were made
  • What known limitations exist
  • How problems are monitored and resolved

This does not mean memorizing technical information. Developers should simply be comfortable explaining their work to another technical professional.

Do Not Script the Answers

Founders should avoid preparing artificial answers designed to make the technology sound better than it is.

Technical reviewers are usually trying to understand how the engineering organization actually works. Inconsistent or overly rehearsed responses can create more uncertainty than an honest explanation of a known limitation.

Instead, make sure the team understands the facts and can explain them clearly.

Review Technical Debt With the Team

Developers often have a more detailed understanding of technical debt than founders or investors.

Ask the team to identify areas that:

  • Slow development
  • Cause recurring bugs
  • Make testing difficult
  • Create infrastructure problems
  • Limit future product changes
  • Require significant maintenance effort

Compare these findings with the company's existing technical debt register.

Discuss Known Architecture Constraints

The engineering team should understand the limitations of the current architecture.

For example, the team may know that a particular component will need to change when:

  • User volume increases
  • A new product workflow is introduced
  • A major integration is added
  • Enterprise requirements become more demanding

These constraints should be documented rather than left as informal knowledge.

Review the Technical Roadmap

Engineers should understand the relationship between the product roadmap and upcoming technical work.

They should know which initiatives are expected to require:

  • Architecture changes
  • Infrastructure improvements
  • Security work
  • Database changes
  • Additional engineering capacity

This helps demonstrate that engineering priorities are connected to business objectives.

Prepare for Questions About Development Capacity

A technical reviewer may want to understand whether the current team can execute the company's plans.

Be prepared to explain:

  • Current engineering capacity
  • Major projects underway
  • Known bottlenecks
  • Hiring requirements
  • External development support

Founders should avoid presenting an unrealistic roadmap that assumes unlimited engineering capacity.

Review External Development Relationships

If contractors or development agencies contribute to the product, make their responsibilities clear.

The company should understand:

  • What the external team owns
  • What internal employees own
  • Where source code is maintained
  • Who controls production access
  • How technical knowledge is transferred

This is particularly important when an external team controls a significant portion of the product.

Prepare for Questions About Employee Turnover

A reviewer may ask what would happen if an important engineer left the company.

Founders should be able to explain how the startup protects critical knowledge through documentation, shared access, code reviews, and team collaboration.

The goal is to demonstrate that the company can continue operating even when individual team members change.

Conduct a Practice Technical Review

A mock review can help the engineering team become comfortable with the process.

Ask an experienced technical professional to question the team about:

  • Architecture
  • Code quality
  • Security
  • Infrastructure
  • Technical debt
  • Scalability
  • Development processes

The purpose is to identify knowledge gaps and documentation problems, not to test whether developers can perform under pressure.

Give the Team Context About the Review

Engineers should understand why the technical review is happening and what information investors are trying to establish.

This helps prevent unnecessary anxiety and encourages more useful conversations between the engineering team and reviewers.

Address Gaps Before the Review

If the preparation process reveals unclear ownership, missing documentation, access problems, or significant knowledge gaps, address the highest-risk issues first.

Not every problem needs to be solved before due diligence. Some can be documented with an appropriate remediation plan.

Keep the Preparation Honest

The engineering team should not be encouraged to hide technical limitations.

Every startup has constraints. A team that can clearly explain what works, what does not, and what it plans to improve can provide more useful information than one attempting to present an unrealistic picture of technical perfection.

Final Thoughts

Preparing an engineering team for technical due diligence is primarily about clarity.

Founders should establish clear responsibilities, identify knowledge dependencies, review technical processes, confirm access and ownership, and make sure engineers understand the architecture and technical roadmap.

The goal is not to turn developers into spokespeople for the company. It is to ensure that the people closest to the technology can explain how the product works, why important decisions were made, and where meaningful technical risks remain.

That preparation can make the review more efficient while giving investors a clearer understanding of the engineering organization behind the product.
