How US Startups Can Prepare for a Product Discovery Workshop Before MVP Development

A product discovery workshop can help a startup turn an early product idea into a clearer development plan. It gives founders, product stakeholders, designers, and developers an opportunity to examine the problem, users, workflows, and technical requirements before development begins.

Good preparation makes these sessions more productive. Instead of spending the workshop trying to reconstruct basic information, the team can focus on decisions, assumptions, and questions that need to be resolved.

Define the Purpose of the Workshop

Start by deciding what the workshop needs to accomplish.

Depending on the project, the objectives may include:

  • Clarifying the target users
  • Defining the core problem
  • Mapping the primary user journey
  • Identifying MVP features
  • Discussing technical requirements
  • Identifying unknowns and risks
  • Creating an initial development scope

A clear objective prevents the discussion from becoming a general conversation about the product.

Prepare the Product Background

Give participants enough context before the workshop.

This may include a short description of the business, the problem being addressed, the intended users, existing solutions, and what the startup hopes to achieve with the MVP.

The material does not need to be a large presentation. A concise document can give everyone a common starting point.

Define the Target Users

The team should have a working definition of who the MVP is intended for.

Describe the user's role, relevant characteristics, current workflow, and the circumstances in which they experience the problem.

If there are multiple user groups, identify which group is the primary audience for the first release.

Describe the Core Problem

Explain the problem from the user's perspective rather than starting with the proposed software.

Discuss what users currently do, where difficulties occur, and what consequences result from the problem.

This gives the workshop a foundation for deciding which product capabilities are actually necessary.

Map the Primary User Journey

Outline the main sequence of actions a user is expected to take.

For example, the journey might involve creating an account, entering information, completing a task, reviewing the result, and taking a follow-up action.

The purpose is not to document every possible scenario. Focus on the primary workflow that the MVP needs to support.

List Known Features

Prepare a preliminary feature list before the workshop.

Group features into categories such as:

  • Essential MVP functionality
  • Supporting functionality
  • Potential future features
  • Unknown or undecided requirements

This gives the team something concrete to evaluate instead of trying to create the entire product scope from scratch during the discussion.

Identify Existing Systems and Tools

If the startup already uses software, identify it before the workshop.

This could include customer relationship systems, payment platforms, databases, internal tools, analytics systems, or other business software.

The team can then determine which existing systems need to interact with the MVP and which processes can remain separate during the initial release.

Prepare Important Business Rules

Some product behavior depends on rules that are not obvious from the feature list.

For example, different users may have different permissions, certain actions may only be available after a specific event, or particular records may need to follow defined status transitions.

Document known rules so they can be discussed during the workshop rather than discovered unexpectedly during development.

Bring Design References

If the startup has examples of products it likes, collect them before the workshop.

These references can communicate preferences around navigation, layouts, interactions, and overall product experience.

They should be treated as references rather than instructions to reproduce another product.

Identify Technical Constraints

Share technical requirements or restrictions that are already known.

These might involve existing infrastructure, required platforms, security requirements, preferred technologies, data sources, or deployment environments.

Known constraints can affect the feasibility and scope of different product decisions.

Prepare Questions, Not Just Answers

A discovery workshop should also expose uncertainty.

Create a list of questions where the team does not yet have a clear answer.

These might concern:

  • User permissions
  • Data ownership
  • Business rules
  • Third-party services
  • Edge cases
  • Future expansion
  • Technical limitations

Clearly identifying uncertainty is more useful than pretending every requirement has already been decided.

Bring the Right Decision Makers

The workshop should include people who can provide relevant information and make product decisions.

Too many participants can make discussions difficult to manage, while missing decision makers can result in unresolved questions.

Before the session, determine who is responsible for final product decisions and make sure that person is involved.

Separate Requirements From Ideas

During discovery, participants may suggest additional features or future possibilities.

Keep these ideas separate from confirmed MVP requirements.

A feature can be recorded as a potential idea without immediately becoming part of the development scope.

Define the Expected Output

Decide what should exist after the workshop.

Possible outputs include:

  • Defined MVP scope
  • User journey map
  • Feature requirements
  • Technical requirements
  • Open questions
  • Product assumptions
  • Initial development milestones

Having a defined output makes it easier to determine whether the workshop achieved its purpose.

Document Decisions During the Session

Important decisions should be recorded while the workshop is taking place.

Capture what was agreed, what remains unresolved, and who is responsible for answering outstanding questions.

This creates a reference point for the development team and reduces the risk of different participants remembering decisions differently.

Review the Results Before Development

Do not move directly from the workshop into development without reviewing the resulting requirements.

Give stakeholders an opportunity to confirm that the documented scope accurately represents the discussion.

Resolving misunderstandings at this stage is generally easier than correcting them after development has already started.

Final Thoughts

A product discovery workshop works best when participants arrive with enough information to make meaningful decisions while remaining open about what is still unknown.

US startups can prepare by defining the workshop objectives, documenting the target users and core problem, mapping the primary workflow, preparing feature ideas, identifying technical constraints, and recording important questions.

The resulting clarity can provide a stronger foundation for defining an MVP that is focused on the product's most important requirements rather than an expanding list of assumptions.
