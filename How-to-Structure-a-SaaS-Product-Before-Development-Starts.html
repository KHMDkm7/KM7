How to Structure a SaaS Product Before Development Starts

A SaaS product may begin with a simple idea, but turning that idea into working software requires several decisions. Founders need to understand the customer problem, define the main user experience, determine the essential functionality, and establish what the first release should accomplish.

Planning these elements before development starts can reduce ambiguity and make it easier for everyone involved to work toward the same product goal. It also gives founders a clearer basis for managing scope, timelines, and development priorities.

Define the Problem the Product Will Solve

The first part of the product plan should explain the problem from the customer's perspective. A broad description of the software is not enough. The team needs to understand what users currently struggle with and what the proposed product should improve.

Consider questions such as:

  • Who experiences the problem?
  • How is the problem handled today?
  • What makes the current process inefficient?
  • How frequently does the problem occur?
  • What outcome would users consider valuable?

A specific problem statement gives the product a clear direction. It also becomes a useful reference when deciding whether new features deserve a place in the initial release.

Identify the Users and Their Responsibilities

Understanding who will use the product is essential before defining its functionality. A SaaS application may have one primary user or several user types with different responsibilities.

For each important user, establish what they need to accomplish and what information or functionality they require.

  • Account owners may manage subscriptions and settings.
  • Employees may perform day-to-day tasks.
  • Managers may review activity and approve actions.
  • Customers may access specific information or submit requests.
  • Administrators may manage users and system settings.

These distinctions can influence navigation, permissions, account structures, and other parts of the product architecture.

Map the Core User Journey

Once the users are defined, map the most important workflow they will follow inside the product. Focus on the journey that delivers the product's primary value.

For example, a SaaS platform might have a workflow like this:

  1. The user creates an account.
  2. The user completes the initial setup.
  3. The user enters the required information.
  4. The user performs the primary action.
  5. The system processes the request.
  6. The user receives and reviews the result.

Following the journey step by step can reveal requirements that may not appear in a simple feature list. Authentication, notifications, permissions, data validation, and integrations may all become necessary depending on how the workflow operates.

Prioritize the Features for the First Release

Not every feature in the long-term product vision needs to be developed immediately. Separating essential functionality from future ideas can keep the initial project focused.

Core Features

These are required for users to complete the primary workflow and experience the main value of the product.

Supporting Features

These improve usability or add convenience but may not be essential for the first release.

Future Features

These are potential additions that can be evaluated after the product has been tested with real users.

For example, advanced analytics, extensive integrations, custom automation, and mobile applications may be part of the long-term vision without being necessary for the initial launch.

Review the Technical Requirements

Product decisions can have direct technical consequences. Features involving payments, multiple organizations, external APIs, real-time communication, or complex permissions may require additional architecture and development work.

Important areas to consider include:

  • Authentication and account management
  • User roles and permissions
  • Database and data storage requirements
  • Payment processing
  • Third-party integrations
  • File management
  • Security requirements
  • Hosting and deployment

The goal is to choose a technical approach that fits the product's actual needs rather than introducing unnecessary complexity.

Establish Clear Boundaries for Development

A product plan should specify both what will be built and what will remain outside the initial scope. This creates a practical boundary for the development team.

For example, the first release might include:

  • User registration and authentication
  • The primary product workflow
  • Basic user management
  • Essential notifications
  • Simple reporting

Advanced reporting, additional integrations, mobile applications, and complex automation can remain on the future roadmap.

Documenting these exclusions is useful because it prevents features from gradually entering the project without a clear discussion of their impact on development effort.

Turn the Plan Into a Development Brief

Once the major decisions have been made, bring them together into a practical document that the development team can use as a reference.

A useful brief can include:

  • Product overview
  • Problem statement
  • Target users
  • Core user journeys
  • Feature priorities
  • User roles
  • Technical requirements
  • Integrations
  • Initial scope
  • Future product ideas

The document does not need to describe every technical implementation. Its primary purpose is to remove ambiguity and give founders, designers, and developers the same understanding of what is being built.

Review the Scope Before Development Begins

Before development starts, review the product plan for missing requirements, unnecessary features, unclear workflows, and technical dependencies.

This is also the right time to discuss the scope with the development team. Developers may identify technical implications that are not obvious from a business perspective, giving founders an opportunity to simplify or reprioritize requirements before work begins.

Once development is underway, new ideas should be evaluated against the original product objectives. A feature can still be added when justified, but its effect on scope, cost, dependencies, and timelines should be understood first.

Conclusion

Structuring a SaaS product before development starts gives founders a clearer path from concept to execution. Defining the customer problem, users, core workflow, feature priorities, and technical requirements creates a shared foundation for the project.

The objective is not to predict every feature the product will ever need. It is to establish a focused first release that can deliver meaningful value and provide useful feedback for future development.
